How it all began: the history of computers

Everything you will learn in this series was invented by people, one step at a time, over about 80 years. This first lesson tells that story: from wartime machines that filled a room, to the chip in a Raspberry Pi, to AI you can talk to in plain English. Click the events to read more.

How to learn in the AI era. In this AI era, you only need to understand a bit of each concept and the big picture. Then add a lot of imagination, and do a lot of brainstorming with AI.

A live lesson will walk you through real case studies to show that, once you get the concept and the big picture, it's surprisingly easy to come up with a Physical AI system. Some of these designs can be finished in a few hours with our software and hardware tools. The founder of DiGiKits Inc. has already put together a system to make your job easy.

One balance to keep: for design and brainstorming, the big picture is enough. For customer support and safety, know the basics cold, because a customer with a stopped machine needs a right answer, not a guess. Most support and software roles at DiGiKits won't do much field or hardware work themselves, and DiGiKits builds internal tools to do the heavy lifting. But you still need to understand the hardware side, so you can help customers clearly and talk easily with the hardware team.

Self-paced, so go easy on yourself. This is a story lesson. You don't need to memorise dates; aim to understand how each step made the next one possible. Write your favourite surprise on the board below. Your coach shares more stories in the live session.

Four kinds of switch

Here is the key idea of this whole series: a computer is a huge number of switches (Lesson 1 shows how). The whole history of computers is mostly the story of the switch getting smaller, faster, cheaper and more reliable.

The timeline

Click any event to read more
  1. 1804Punched cards control a weaving loomidea

    Joseph-Marie Jacquard's loom in France read a chain of cards with holes. A hole or no hole decided whether a thread was lifted. That's a 1 or a 0, more than 100 years before computers.

  2. 1830s–40sBabbage and Lovelace imagine a programmable machinesoftware

    Charles Babbage designed the Analytical Engine, a mechanical computer that would read punched cards. It was never finished. Ada Lovelace wrote what's often called the first computer program for it, and saw that such machines could one day work with music and pictures, not only numbers.

  3. 1890Punched cards count the US censushardware

    Herman Hollerith's card-reading machines sped up the census. His company later became part of IBM, and punched cards stayed the main way to feed computers until the 1970s.

  4. 1936Alan Turing describes a universal machineidea

    Turing showed on paper that one machine, following simple rules from a tape, could do any calculation that can be written as steps. Every computer since is a “universal machine” in his sense.

  5. 1941Z3: a programmable computer built from relaysrelays

    Konrad Zuse in Germany built the Z3 from about 2,600 telephone relays, the same kind of switch you will meet in Lesson 3. It read its program from holes punched in old movie film.

  6. 1943–44Colossus: electronic code-breaking in WWIItubes

    At Bletchley Park in Britain, Tommy Flowers built Colossus from about 1,600 vacuum tubes (later versions about 2,400) to help break German codes. It read messages from punched paper tape at high speed. It was kept secret until the 1970s, so for years few people knew it existed.

  7. 1944Harvard Mark I: a 15-metre relay machinerelays

    An electromechanical calculator about 15 metres long, fed by punched paper tape. Grace Hopper was one of its first programmers.

  8. 1945–46ENIAC: the first general-purpose electronic computertubes

    Built in the USA for wartime calculations and finished just after the war. It had about 17,500 vacuum tubes, weighed about 27 tonnes and filled a large room. It was programmed by plugging cables and setting switches by hand, a job done mostly by six women programmers. Changing the program could take days.

  9. 1947The transistor is invented, and the first “bug”transistor

    At Bell Labs, Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley made the first transistor: a switch with no moving parts and no glowing bulb. The same year, operators of the Harvard Mark II taped a moth found in a relay into their logbook as the “first actual case of bug being found”.

  10. 1950sTransistor computers replace tubestransistor

    Transistors were smaller, cooler and far more reliable than tubes, which burned out like light bulbs. By the late 1950s, big commercial computers were built from them.

  11. 1952–57Compilers and FORTRAN: the first popular high-level languagesoftware

    Grace Hopper built one of the first compilers (1952), proving a computer could translate human-friendly code into machine code. IBM's FORTRAN (1957), led by John Backus, became the first widely used high-level language. COBOL followed in 1959. This is the first rung up the ladder you will see in Lesson 2.

  12. 1958–59The integrated circuit: many transistors on one chipchip

    Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce at Fairchild separately found ways to build several transistors and their wiring on one piece of material. Every chip today descends from this.

  13. 1965Moore's lawidea

    Gordon Moore noticed the number of transistors on a chip was doubling regularly, and predicted it would continue. It held, roughly every two years, for decades. See the chart below.

  14. 1969ARPANET and Unixsoftware

    ARPANET, the network that grew into the Internet, sent its first message. The same year, work began on Unix at Bell Labs. Linux, which runs on your Pi, follows Unix's ideas.

  15. 1971Intel 4004: the first commercial microprocessorchip

    A whole CPU on one chip, with about 2,300 transistors, made for a calculator. It needed separate memory chips around it.

  16. 1972The C languagesoftware

    Dennis Ritchie created C at Bell Labs to write Unix. Most firmware for microcontrollers is still written in C today.

  17. 1974–76Microcontrollers: a computer on one chipchip

    Texas Instruments' TMS1000 (1974) put CPU, memory and input/output on one chip: the first widely sold microcontroller (MCU). Intel's 8048 (1976) and 8051 (1980) followed and were used in billions of products. The RP2350 on DiGiKits boards is their great-grandchild.

  18. 1976Single-board computers with a keypadhardware

    Boards like the KIM-1 had a hex keypad and a row of 7-segment LED digits. You typed your program in, byte by byte, in hex machine code, and read the results on the LEDs. See the “programming through the ages” drawing below.

  19. 1977Personal computers arrive at homehardware

    The Apple II, Commodore PET and TRS-80 came with a keyboard and a screen, and the BASIC language built in. For the first time, ordinary people could program at home.

  20. 1981–84The IBM PC and the mouse-driven Macintoshhardware

    The IBM PC (1981) set the standard most office computers followed. The Macintosh (1984) made windows, icons and a mouse popular, ideas first developed at Xerox PARC.

  21. 1989–91The World Wide Websoftware

    Tim Berners-Lee at CERN invented web pages, links and HTTP, one of the protocols in Lesson 7. The web opened to the public in 1991.

  22. 1991Linux and Pythonsoftware

    Linus Torvalds released Linux, the free operating system on your Pi. Guido van Rossum released Python, the language you use in these lessons. Both are free and open source.

  23. 2005Arduinohardware

    A cheap, friendly microcontroller board from Italy that made electronics easy for students and makers. It started the maker movement DiGiKits grew up in.

  24. 2007The smartphone erahardware

    The iPhone, and Android soon after, put a powerful computer, a camera, GPS and sensors in every pocket.

  25. 2012Raspberry Pi, and the deep-learning breakthroughAI

    The Raspberry Pi launched as a $35 computer for learning. The same year, a neural network called AlexNet won an image-recognition contest by a wide margin, starting the modern AI boom.

  26. 2017The transformerAI

    Google researchers published “Attention Is All You Need”, describing the transformer. Almost every large language model today, Claude included, is built on this idea.

  27. 2021–24Raspberry Pi's own chips: RP2040 and RP2350chip

    Raspberry Pi designed its own microcontrollers: the RP2040 on the Pico (2021) and the RP2350 (2024), used in DiGiKits designs.

  28. 2022–23AI you can talk toAI

    ChatGPT (late 2022) and Claude (2023) let anyone use AI in plain English. The top rung of the ladder in Lesson 2 arrived: you describe what you want, and AI writes the code.

  29. TodayPhysical AIAI

    AI is moving off the screen and into machines that sense and act in the real world: robots, smart controllers, inspection cameras. That's the loop you will learn in Lesson 1, and it's what DiGiKits builds. You're joining the story right here.

How many switches? A chart

Each step on this chart is about ten times more than the one below it. A chart like this, where each line is ten times the last, is called a log scale; without it the early machines would be invisible.

Programming through the ages

Here's the same tiny job, add 2 and 3, done the way each generation did it. Step through and watch programming climb, one layer at a time. (Lesson 2 explains these layers.)

The big lesson from history

Every generation took the hard, low-level work and hid it under a new layer, so the next generation could think bigger. Relays became tubes, tubes became transistors, transistors became chips. Plugboards became punched cards, cards became hex keypads, keypads became FORTRAN, FORTRAN became Python, and Python is becoming plain English. By the people, for the people, all the way through. In the AI era, your job is to understand the layers well enough to imagine what to build next.
