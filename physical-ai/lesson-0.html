Physical AI Systems · Lesson 0

How it all began: the real “language” computers use

Every computer, every Raspberry Pi, every LED controller and every AI (Claude included) comes down to the same simple thing: tiny switches that are either on or off. This lesson shows how electricity becomes 0s and 1s, and how 0s and 1s become the things a Physical AI system senses and does.

Electricity is the medium, binary is the code

People often say “computers think in electrical pulses” or “computers speak binary.” Both are half true, and the two halves fit together:

  • Electricity is the medium. Inside the chip, data is a voltage on a wire: high or low. The voltage is the physical thing you could measure with a meter.
  • Binary is the code. We agree that a high voltage means 1 and a low voltage means 0. Each 0 or 1 is called a bit; eight bits make a byte.

So the medium is electricity, and the information it carries is written in binary. Outside the chip, the same bits can travel in other media too: as light in an optical fibre, as radio waves in Wi-Fi, or as tiny magnetic spots on an old hard drive.

Why only two states?

A transistor works best as a switch: fully on or fully off. Using just two states has big advantages:

  • Noise doesn't matter much. A signal can sag or wobble a little and still clearly read as high or low. With ten levels (for decimal digits) a small wobble would turn a 6 into a 7.
  • It's electrically simple and reliable. On/off switches are easy to build, tiny, and use little power.
  • It scales. A modern chip holds billions of transistors switching billions of times per second.

Everything else, like numbers, letters, colours, sound and pictures, must be encoded into binary before the hardware can work with it.

Binary is the alphabet, not the whole language. The 0s and 1s are like letters. The real “language” a processor understands is its machine code: a fixed set of instructions (add these, compare those, jump there), each one written as a pattern of bits. A Raspberry Pi's ARM processor and a laptop's Intel processor use different machine codes, which is why a program built for one won't run on the other without being rebuilt.

How it works in a Physical AI system

The real world is mostly analog: temperature, light, sound and position change smoothly, not in steps. A Physical AI system has to cross the border between the analog world and the digital computer twice, once coming in and once going out.

  1. Sense. A sensor such as a microphone, thermometer or light sensor produces an analog voltage that rises and falls with the real world. (A keyboard or mouse is different: it already sends digital data over USB or Bluetooth.)
  2. Convert in. An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) measures that voltage many times per second and turns each measurement into a binary number.
  3. Process. The processor's logic gates work on those binary numbers, all carried as high and low voltages through the circuit. This is where a program, or an AI, decides what to do.
  4. Convert out. A digital-to-analog converter (DAC), or a fast on/off trick called PWM, turns binary back into something the world can feel: a sound wave, a motor speed, an LED brightness.
  5. Act. The speaker plays, the motor turns, the LED glows. You'll do exactly this with the LED strip in Phase 1, Day 8.

Hands-on

1. Count to 31 on one hand

Each finger is a bit: up = 1, down = 0. Thumb is worth 1, index 2, middle 4, ring 8, little finger 16. Show 5 (thumb + middle), then 19, then 31. Two hands go up to 1023.

2. Your initial in binary

Look up your first initial in an ASCII table (search “ASCII table”). Write its code number, then its 8 bits. Check yourself on the Pi or any Linux terminal:

echo -n A | xxd -b      # shows 01000001
printf '%d\n' "'A"       # shows 65

3. Spot the border
