Physical AI Systems · Lesson 4

Sensors and the ADC: how the real world becomes numbers

A Physical AI system starts by sensing: how warm is it, how bright, how far, how fast? The world gives smooth, analog answers. The computer only understands numbers made of bits. The part that crosses that border is the analog-to-digital converter, or ADC. Move the sliders and watch it work.

Where this fits in the loop

From a voltage to a number

Many sensors turn what they measure into a voltage. A light sensor might give 0 V in the dark and 3.3 V in bright sun. The ADC measures that voltage and gives back a whole number. A 12-bit ADC, like the one in the RP2350 microcontroller, gives numbers from 0 to 4095.

ADC number = voltage ÷ 3.3 × 4095. The program never sees the voltage, only this number.

Two choices every ADC makes

An ADC can't watch a signal continuously. It takes a sample (one measurement) again and again. Two settings decide how good the copy is:

  • Sample rate: how many measurements per second. Too few, and fast changes are missed.
  • Resolution (bits): how many steps it can tell apart. 3 bits give 8 steps; 12 bits give 4096.

The dashed line is the real signal. The blue staircase is what the computer gets. Make it terrible, then make it good.

Same idea everywhere. A phone microphone samples sound about 48,000 times per second at 16 bits or more. A thermostat might sample once a second, because room temperature changes slowly. Choosing “fast enough and fine enough, but no more” is an everyday engineering decision.

Important for support: the Pi has no ADC

A Raspberry Pi's GPIO pins are digital only: each one reads just high or low. To read an analog sensor, a DiGiKits system uses one of three routes:

What the customer seesLikely causeFirst check
Reading stuck at 0Sensor not powered, or signal wire loosePower and wiring; measure the voltage with a meter
Reading stuck at maximum (4095 or 65535)Signal wire touching the supply, or the sensor's ground wire missingWiring and ground; measure the signal voltage with a meter
Reading jumps around a lotSignal wire disconnected (a “floating” input), electrical noise, long wiresShorter wires, common ground, average several samples
“No device” on the I²C busWrong address or wiring (SDA/SCL swapped)i2cdetect -y 1 on the Pi to list addresses

Safety for the hardware: never connect more than 3.3 V to an ADC or GPIO pin on a Pi, Pico or RP2350. A 5 V or 12 V sensor needs a voltage divider or a level shifter first, or the pin (and sometimes the whole chip) is damaged.

Hands-on

1. Do the ADC's job

For a 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V range, work out the number for 0 V, 0.825 V, 1.65 V and 3.3 V. Then check with the slider above. (Hint: they're a quarter apart.)

2. Break the copy

In the sampling explorer, find the lowest sample rate and the fewest bits where you can still recognise the wave. Post your two numbers on the board below.

3. Read a real sensor (with your coach or after Phase 2, Day 7)

On a Pico or RP2350 board with a knob (potentiometer) wired to pin GP26, in MicroPython:

from machine import ADC
import time

knob = ADC(26)                     # GP26 is ADC input 0
while True:
    raw = knob.read_u16()          # MicroPython scales it to 0–65535
    volts = raw * 3.3 / 65535
    print(raw, round(volts, 2), "V")
    time.sleep(0.5)

Turn the knob and watch the numbers follow.
