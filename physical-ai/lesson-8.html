How DiGiKits uses Claude: making tools that make tools

You've seen how computers grew, layer by layer, from switches to plain English. This lesson shows how DiGiKits works on the very top layer every day: the founder and the team work with Claude, an AI made by Anthropic, as a teammate. And the most powerful habit isn't asking Claude to do a job once. It's using Claude to build tools, and then tools that build more tools.

Self-paced, so go easy on yourself. This lesson is about a way of working, not facts to memorise. Try the drawings, then try the hands-on with Claude yourself. The live session shows real examples of DiGiKits tools in action.

Claude as a teammate

At DiGiKits, people and AI have different jobs. The founder works as the planner and product manager: deciding what to build, for whom, and why. Claude works like a tireless developer, system architect and team leader who is available at any hour: drafting code, designs, documents and plans, and asking hard questions back.

People decideClaude drafts and helpsTests and hardware have the final word
What to build and whyCode, firmware, scriptsDoes it pass the automatic checks?
What customers are promisedDesigns, mock-ups, 3D conceptsDoes it work on the real board?
Safety, money and anything that can't be undoneDocuments, guides, training (like this site)Does a person who checked it agree?

Claude is told to push back. A good teammate doesn't just say yes. DiGiKits asks Claude to question ideas, point out mistakes and suggest what's been missed, even the founder's. Many lessons on this site were improved exactly that way: the founder wrote a draft, and Claude corrected facts and added what was missing.

The big idea: tools that make tools

There are four levels of using AI. Most people stay on level 1. DiGiKits works mostly on levels 3 and 4, because that's where one hour of work keeps paying back for years. Click each step, or press Play.

How you'll use Claude in support

  • Understand the problem: paste an error message or log lines and ask what they mean and what to check first.
  • Draft the reply: ask for a clear, friendly answer at the customer's level, then read it, fix it and send it yourself.
  • Write it down: once a case is solved, ask Claude to turn your notes into a knowledge-base article, so the next case is faster. That's level 3 in action.

The rules that never change

  • You own the answer. Claude can be wrong, sometimes confidently. Check facts, voltages, commands and anything safety-related before you act on them or send them.
  • Keep private things private. Never paste passwords, keys, customer personal details or company secrets into any tool unless the company has approved it for that.
  • People make the decisions that affect safety, money, customers or anything that can't be undone.
  • Say what you used. If AI helped write something important, the reviewer should know.

Asking well

Claude gives much better help when you give it what a new colleague would need. Compare:

Weak requestStrong request
“LED strip not working, help.”“A customer's WS2812 LED strip on a Pi shows the wrong colours: red looks green. It's powered from its own 5 V supply with a shared ground. What are the three most likely causes, in order, and what should I ask the customer to check first? Keep it short.”

The strong one gives context (what, where, what's already known), the goal (likely causes, first check) and the format (three items, short).

Hands-on

1. Ask for a second explanation

Pick the lesson in this series you found hardest. Ask Claude to explain it a different way, with an everyday example. Post the best explanation on the board below.

2. Level up one task

Think of one thing you do again and again (a reply, a check, a report). Write it down at all four levels: what would level 1, 2, 3 and 4 look like? You don't need to build it; the thinking is the point.

3. Rewrite a weak request

Take this weak request: “Pi won't connect, why?” Rewrite it as a strong one with context, goal and format. Try both on Claude and compare the answers.
