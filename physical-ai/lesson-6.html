Actuators: how numbers move the real world

Lesson 5 brought the world into the computer. This lesson sends it back out: lighting an LED, turning a motor, moving an arm. The parts that do this are called actuators, and the trick that controls most of them is called PWM. Move the sliders and watch.

PWM: dimming with only on and off

A GPIO pin can only be fully on (3.3 V) or fully off (0 V). So how do you make an LED glow at half brightness? You switch it on and off very fast, hundreds or thousands of times a second. Your eye, or the motor, can't follow the flicker and only feels the average. The share of time it's on is called the duty cycle. That's PWM: pulse-width modulation.

Many LED controllers store brightness as one byte: 0 = off, 255 = full. That's the same byte from Lesson 2.

The other direction of Lesson 5. The ADC turned a smooth voltage into a number. PWM turns a number back into an average power. Some chips also have a DAC (digital-to-analog converter) that makes a true smooth voltage, used mostly for sound. For lights and motors, PWM is cheaper and works just as well.

Servos: a pulse that means an angle

A hobby servo also listens to pulses, but it reads them differently: about 50 times a second it gets one pulse, and the length of that pulse tells it which angle to turn to and hold. Roughly 1 ms means one end, 2 ms means the other end. (Exact numbers vary by servo; check its datasheet.)

The pin gives the order; a driver does the lifting

A GPIO pin can only supply a tiny current, a few milliamps. That's enough for one small LED, but a motor, a strip of LEDs or a solenoid needs hundreds or thousands of times more. Connecting one straight to a pin can destroy the Pi or the microcontroller. So every real actuator sits behind a driver.

  • Driver: a MOSFET, transistor or motor-driver board switches the heavy power on the pin's command.
  • Separate supply: the load gets its own power supply, sized for it; it doesn't take power from the Pi's 5 V pin.
  • Common ground: the minus side of every supply is joined, so the pin's signal has a reference.
  • Flyback diode: motors, relays and solenoids kick back a voltage spike when switched off. A diode across them (often built into driver boards) soaks it up.

Smart LED strips are different. A WS2812 strip, like the one in Phase 1, Day 8, has a tiny chip in every LED. The data wire carries colour bytes (a protocol, coming in Lesson 7), and each chip does its own PWM. The strip still needs its own power supply and a common ground.

Safety first: actuators move and get hot. Power off before touching wiring, keep fingers clear of motors and arms that might start, and never work on anything connected to mains (120 V wall power). That's for a licensed electrician; see Phase 2, Day 14.

Hands-on

1. Duty cycle maths

At 3.3 V, what average voltage do you get at 25%, 50% and 75% duty? What 8-bit value is each? Check with the slider.

2. Fade an LED on the Pi

With the LED on GPIO17 from Phase 1, Day 7:

from gpiozero import PWMLED
from time import sleep

led = PWMLED(17)
for step in range(0, 101, 10):
    led.value = step / 100        # duty cycle from 0.0 to 1.0
    print(step, "%")
    sleep(0.5)
led.pulse()                       # breathe in and out on its own
input("Press Enter to stop")
